# Research Briefing: Did the US–China Tariffs Reroute World Trade — or Shrink It?

## The 10-Minute Version

**What they're chasing:** when America taxes Chinese goods, does the world's trade find a new
route — through Vietnam, Mexico, Taiwan — or does some of it just disappear, and who ends up
better off?

## Three Questions You Can Ask

Ask as a genuinely curious person who doesn't know the subject — because you are one. **You need no
economics for any of these.** If they stall, *"I still don't follow"* beats a hint. Hedging is a
good sign, not a bad one.

**1. "I keep hearing Vietnam sells way more to America since the China tariffs. Is that Vietnam
winning, or is it just China's stuff taking a detour?"**
*Telling:* "trade diverted to Vietnam," one fluent sentence, over in ten seconds.
*Building:* they separate the cases — part detour (Chinese parts flowing through), part real gain
(Vietnamese wages and factories growing) — and admit the split is hard to measure.
*If it stalls:* "So if I'm Vietnam's government, am I happy about this or nervous?"

**2. "Who ended up actually paying the tariff money — China, American companies, or people
like me?"**
*Telling:* "consumers pay, companies pass it on" — instant, textbook-shaped.
*Building:* they mention what was *measured* — prices at the border before the tariff gets added —
and can say what the data would have looked like if China had paid.
*If it stalls:* "How could anyone possibly know that? What would you measure?"

**3. "So after all your reading — did the tariffs work? What were they even supposed to do?"**
*Telling:* "economists say tariffs are bad" or "everyone lost." Tidy and one-sided.
*Building:* they split the goals — prices, jobs, dependence, politics — and score them separately,
including at least one goal the tariffs arguably achieved.
*If it stalls:* "Say the best case FOR the tariffs, as if you believed it."

## What Good Engagement Looks Like

**Signs they actually read it** — they distinguish the 2018–19 tariffs from the 2025 round without
being asked (Q1) · they can say what a study measured, not just what it concluded (Q6) · they
complain that the UN study and the AER study seem to contradict each other — good, that collision
IS Q5 · they bring up Vietnamese factories buying Chinese parts (Q4).

**Signs they skimmed** — confident percentages with no idea what they're a percentage of · every
source seems to agree, which means Q5 was never opened, because its three sources visibly don't ·
"tariffs are bad for everyone," which none of these studies actually says — the AER paper found
bystander countries gained · no mention of any year or episode.

**Signs it's going well even though it looks like it isn't** — **"this is confusing" after Q4 is
the correct response**; that card is genuinely hard and its conclusion is assembled, never stated ·
changing their mind between Q2 and Q5 · getting stuck on whether two studies contradict each
other — that stuck-ness is the load-bearing card working.

## Words You Might Hear

| Word | What it means |
|---|---|
| **Tariff** | A tax a government charges on goods entering the country |
| **Trade diversion** | Trade that moves to a new country instead of disappearing |
| **Bystander country** | A country not in the fight — Vietnam, Mexico — affected anyway |
| **Pass-through** | How much of a new cost reaches the next buyer down the chain. **Worth them knowing properly** — it applies to every tax and cost shock |
| **Reallocation** | Supply chains reorganising — factories and orders moving countries |
| **Decoupling** | Two economies deliberately reducing their dependence on each other |
| **Retaliation** | China taxing American goods back — it hit US farmers hardest |
| **Truce** | The current pause: both sides holding tariffs at ~10% until November 2026 |

### What We Changed About the Question — short form

**It can be answered without opening a source.** "It affects prices, trade and tensions" is
available from general knowledge, making the sources decoration. **"The world" is five topics
wearing one word** — prices, supply chains, third countries, macro, geopolitics; each is a book.
**"The tariff" is at least three different policies** — the 2018–19 trade war, the 2025
escalation, and the current truce — and findings about one don't transfer to the others. **It
invites a catalogue, not an argument.**

*What changed:* narrowed to the one place the evidence genuinely disagrees — what happened to
everyone who wasn't the US or China. "Reroute or shrink" forces a position; "who came out ahead"
forces separating winners from losers instead of averaging them. The full six-criteria record is
in the appendix.

### The Question Set

*(9 cards; the opener, the hardest, and the load-bearing one shown — full set in the artifact)*

> **Q1 · MEANING** — *the opener*
> "The US tariff on China" — which one? The 2018–19 trade war, the 2025 escalation, or the truce
> running now? Are these one policy that grew, or three policies sharing a name?
> **Why this card** *(for you, not them)*: nearly all the measured evidence is from 2018–19; the
> 2025 round was far bigger. A student who never separates the episodes will quote 2019 findings
> as if they describe 2026.
> **Source:** Bown, PIIE chart · T2 · 20 min · **Check first:** search PIIE's funding and trade
> stance before reading — then read anyway. · **Read for:** the timeline's shape, not the
> percentages. · **They might say** *(for you)*: "It's all one policy" → "The rate went 3% → 21%
> → 40%+ → 10%. When does a policy stop being the same policy?"

> **Q4 · MECHANISM** — *the hardest card*
> If a component that used to ship from China now ships from Vietnam — but the Vietnamese factory
> buys its parts from China — has the world's dependence on China gone down, or just become harder
> to see?
> **Why this card** *(for you, not them)*: Alfaro & Chor show US direct sourcing from China fell
> while Vietnam's and Mexico's imports FROM China rose. The conclusion is assembled from evidence,
> never stated as a slogan. Expect visible frustration — that's the design working.
> **Source:** Alfaro & Chor (2023) · T1 · 50 min · **Check first:** search what Jackson Hole is
> and who presents there. · **Read for:** where Vietnam and Mexico get THEIR inputs — and also
> the evidence that some reallocation is real. Both are in there.
> **They might say** *(for you)*: "It's fake, everything's still Chinese underneath" → "Wages and
> capacity rose in Vietnam too — would that happen if it were pure relabelling?"

> **Q5 · TENSION** — *the load-bearing card*
> Two serious teams measured the same episode. One concludes bystanders won net new trade; a UN
> study concludes the world lost ~$14bn net; the IMF warns continued fragmentation makes everyone
> poorer. Where exactly do they disagree — the numbers, or what they counted?
> **Why this card** *(for you, not them)*: the disagreement is real and empirical — time windows,
> "trade to the US" vs "trade to the world," baselines. If they can state the position they reject
> fairly, the hardest thing in this set is done.
> **Source:** S2 + S4 + S7 (S7 is the unconfirmed one) · mostly re-reading · 45 min
> **Check first:** search each institution's name plus "criticism" — find one informed critic of
> each before picking a side. · **Read for:** the definitions, not the conclusions. Three-row
> table: window, definition of trade, baseline.
> **They might say** *(for you)*: "The UN one is right, it's neutral" → "Neutral about what? What
> did it count that the others didn't?"

The remaining six cards: Q2 asks whether the first-year export surges were new trade or detoured
trade (UNCTAD); Q3 asks why Vietnam and Mexico gained but India didn't (AER: Insights); Q6 asks
what economists measured to conclude Americans paid the tariffs (JEP); Q7 asks which 2018-era
findings survive a world where nearly everyone is tariffed (CRS timeline); Q8 asks what tariffs
are for, given they failed on jobs but won votes (Autor et al.); Q9 is a no-reading thinking card
about who should act differently depending on the answer.

### Checking Our Homework

AI-generated citations fail in a specific way: **a real author, a real venue, and a study that
never existed.** That is more dangerous than obvious fabrication, because searching the author
finds genuine work and the check feels passed. The two-move check: *does it exist?* then *does it
say what we claimed?*

**Start with S7** — it is our one admitted gap, described above. If you find we've attributed the
figure to the wrong IMF document, show the teen; that is the lesson working, not failing.

Topic-specific warning: on this topic the bigger risk is not fake citations but **real numbers
detached from their scope** — "$14bn lost" (first half of 2019 only, goods only), "25% drop"
(tariffed products only, not all trade), "47.5% average tariff" (a specific date, since changed).
If a number appears in the essay without a year and a scope attached, it has come loose.

Disclosure: all eight sources were verified by search — titles, authors, years, venues, and the
claims we attach to them at abstract/summary level. None was read end-to-end. Current-status
facts (the truce, the 10% rate, the November 2026 expiry) were cross-confirmed against multiple
reports on 2026-07-27 and will go stale.

### What This Skill Will Not Do

| Component | AI role |
|---|---|
| Finding and verifying sources | **AI-beneficial** — done, and shown |
| Sharpening the question | **AI-beneficial** — done, with the reasoning preserved |
| Reading the sources | **AI-undermining** — the entire point; not done here |
| Deciding "reroute or shrink" | **AI-undermining** — that judgment IS the work |
| Explaining a concept when genuinely stuck | **AI-neutral** — fine, if the reading came first |
| Writing or outlining the essay | **AI-undermining** — refused |

There is no deadline here, so the temptation is not "get it written" — it is **"get it explained
to me."** A teen who has an AI summarise all eight sources will sound fluent and have learned
nothing; the tell is fluency about conclusions with silence about measurements. **No essay, no
argument outline, no draft, and no telling them which side to take.** The falsifiable test: if
they end up with a position they cannot defend out loud against the three questions above,
something went wrong — and Section 1 is how you find out.
