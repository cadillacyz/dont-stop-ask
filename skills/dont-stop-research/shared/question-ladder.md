# The Question Ladder

Seven question types. Every type must appear at least once in a set.

---

## The seven types

| # | Type | The move it teaches | Paul & Elder (2008) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Meaning** | What are we actually asking? Which words are doing work? | Clarification |
| 2 | **Landscape** | Who has looked at this, what did they find? | Probing reasons and evidence |
| 3 | **Mechanism** | *Why* would that happen? What's the story underneath? | Probing assumptions |
| 4 | **Tension** | Who disagrees, and what is their strongest point? | Viewpoints and perspectives |
| 5 | **Evidence** | How would anyone know? What would count as proof? | Probing reasons and evidence |
| 6 | **Scope** | Where does this stop being true? | Implications and consequences |
| 7 | **Stake** | Who cares, what changes if the answer flips? | Questions about the question |

**Weighting.** Weight toward **Type 3 (Mechanism)** and **Type 4 (Tension)** — the two places research
reliably dies, at every level of expertise. Readers collect *what* and never reach *why*; and a set
with no real disagreement in it produces a summary rather than a position, regardless of how many
sources it cites.

**Coverage rule.** Every one of the seven must appear at least once. This is the constraint that
makes the set-size ceiling safe: you may generate six cards instead of nine, but not six cards that
skip Tension.

---

## Type 3 — Mechanism: use the elaborative forms

Pressley et al. (1992) found explanation-generation substantially outperforms re-reading (d ≈ 0.59).
The gain comes from *generating* the explanation, so the prompt form matters:

- **Causal why** — "Why does X happen?"
- **Contrastive why** — "Why does X happen but not Y?" *(strongest form; use it when theory and
  measurement disagree)*
- **Mechanism how** — "How does X lead to Y?"
- **Connection** — "How is this like [something they already know]?"
- **Predictive** — "Given X, what would you expect about Z? Why?"

**Calibration constraint (Woloshyn et al., 1994):** elaboration requires enough prior knowledge to
generate a plausible explanation. Someone who knows nothing about the topic cannot elaborate — they
need a Type 1 or Type 2 card first. Never place a Mechanism card ahead of the knowledge it depends
on. The floor moves with the reader's background but never disappears, which is what `context` is
for.

---

## Type 4 — Tension: the load-bearing card

At least one Type 4 card must present a disagreement that is **real** — a position held by serious,
informed people, presented **at its strongest**, not as a strawman to be knocked down.

Two failure modes, both bad:

- **Manufactured controversy** — inventing a dispute where the evidence is one-sided. If a topic has
  no live expert disagreement, **say so explicitly**. That is a finding about the topic, not a
  failure of the set.
- **False balance** — presenting a fringe position as equivalent to a well-supported one. Where a
  disagreement is political rather than empirical, name which kind it is.

The test: could someone who read only the Tension card write a fair summary of the position they
personally disagree with?

---

## Ordering and relevance

Cards are generated in ladder order and consumed in ladder order. The set is cumulative — later
cards may assume the reader has worked the earlier ones, and Scope/Stake cards frequently need no new
source because they run on what the earlier cards established. Say so when that is the case; a card
with no reading attached is a feature, not an omission.

Ladder order is not the same as `relevance_group`. Ladder order is *pedagogical sequence*; relevance
group is *distance from the working question*. A Type 1 Meaning card is early in the sequence and
usually `supporting` in relevance. Both orderings are recorded, and consumers may use either.
