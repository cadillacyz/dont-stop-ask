## Three Questions to Ask (yourself, out loud, after the reading)

These work read aloud to anyone who will listen — the listener needs to know nothing; that is the
design. *"I still don't follow"* from them beats a hint. Hedging is a good sign, not a bad one.

**1. "You keep saying you want the kids to sound more native — native like who? And how would you
know when they'd made it?"**
*Telling:* "like people born here," flat, no split between accent, grammar and idiom.
*Building:* a specific target, an admission the targets differ in movability, and a mention that
serious researchers reject the native yardstick entirely — with an opinion on whether that's wisdom
or bar-lowering.

**2. "If you could only pick one: an hour of English cartoons a day, or ten minutes of real
back-and-forth conversation — which, and why?"**
*Telling:* "conversation, obviously, screens are bad" — the right answer for the wrong reason.
*Building:* citing the live-vs-video experiment and then hedging it properly — that was infants
learning sounds, and what video can't do for a baby it may partly do for a ten-year-old. The hedge
IS the understanding.

**3. "Straight answer: is it too late for them? What's still open at their age and what's mostly
closed?"**
*Telling:* "it's never too late" or "after seven it's over" — either absolute, delivered fast.
*Building:* the split — grammar-learning strong deep into the teens per the biggest study; accent
the stubborn one, set much earlier; and part of the age effect dissolving into education-and-use
when controlled.

## Words You Might Hear (in your own answers)

| Word | What it means |
|---|---|
| **Critical period** | An age window after which some kinds of language learning get harder. How sharp, and for what, is exactly what's disputed |
| **Age of arrival / exposure** | How old the child was when serious English exposure began — the studies' favourite variable |
| **Phonetics** | The sound system — what accent is made of |
| **Morphosyntax** | Grammar in the studies' sense: word forms and sentence structure. **Worth knowing properly** — it's the part that stays open longest |
| **Input** | All the English reaching the child. Quantity and richness are measured separately |
| **Social gating** | The proposal that language learning is unlocked by live human interaction, not by exposure alone |
| **L2 user** | Cook's term: someone who uses a second language in their own right — not a failed native |
| **Dominant language** | The stronger of a bilingual's languages. Most of the world's bilinguals have one |

### What We Changed About the Question — short form

**As asked, it's tip-collection.** Any parenting blog answers it without a single study. **"More
natively" is four targets wearing one word** — accent, grammar, vocabulary, idiom — with different
timetables and different fixes. **It assumes the goal** — whether native-likeness is the right
target for a bilingual child is a live dispute, not a footnote. **No ages given**, and the
evidence differs sharply by age band.

*What changed:* split the movable from the less-movable, and asked what changes what — a question
the evidence genuinely disagrees about, which forces a position instead of a list.

### The Question Set

*(8 cards; the opener, the hardest, and the load-bearing one shown — full set in the artifact)*

> **Q1 · MEANING** — *the opener* — "More natively" — accent, grammar, vocabulary, or idiom? Which
