## Triage record (Stage 0)

Evaluated as stated: *"how to teach my kids to speak English more natively?"*

**THREATS**

1. **Not evidence-dependent as stated.** Any parenting blog answers it — "talk more, read together,
   limit subtitles" — without opening a study. The sources become decoration for advice you already
   had.
2. **"More natively" is four different targets wearing one word.** Accent, grammar, vocabulary, and
   idiom develop on different timetables, respond to different kinds of exposure, and close at
   different ages. A plan that doesn't pick one can't be evaluated.
3. **It assumes the goal.** Whether native-likeness is even the right target for a bilingual child
   is a live, serious dispute in the field — not a fringe objection. The question as stated skips
   the debate the answer depends on.
4. **No ages, no baseline.** The evidence differs sharply by age band: what's true of infant
   phonetics is false of school-age grammar. "My kids" leaves the most decision-relevant variable
   blank.
5. **It invites tip-collection, not judgment.** A list of tips can't conflict with itself, so
   nothing forces weighing — and the real literature conflicts constantly.

**REVISED QUESTION**

> **What kind of English exposure actually changes how my kids speak — and which parts of
> "sounding native" are still movable at their age?**

**WHAT CHANGED AND WHY**

The revision splits "more natively" into movable and less-movable parts (which is where the
research genuinely disagrees), and shifts from "what should I do" to "what changes what" — a
question that cannot be answered without evidence and forces choosing between rival accounts:
more input vs. richer input vs. earlier input, and whether the native-speaker target is even the
right yardstick. The parenting decisions fall out of the answer instead of replacing it.
